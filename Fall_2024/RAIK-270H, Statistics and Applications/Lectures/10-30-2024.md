## Inferential Statistics
We will now be sampling data instead of using population data. <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">This is the basis for inferential statistics</mark>. Just taking a sample and applying it to the whole population

## Uniform Distribution
<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Uniform distribution</mark> is when every value a sample can take is equally weighted. Everything is equally likely to happen

Density of a uniform distribution is just a square. <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">The pdf is</mark>
$$f(x)=\frac{1}{b-a}$$
for a <= x <= b, or 0 otherwise
![[Pasted image 20241030160359.png]]

Expected value:
$$E(X)=\frac{a+b}{2}$$
Variance:
$$Var=E(X^2)-(E(X))^2=\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}$$
Standard deviation:
$$Stdev=\sqrt{\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}}$$

## Central Limit Theorem
The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Central limit theorem</mark> is that the distribution of sample means approximates a normal distribution as the sample size increases, regardless of the population's distribution
	The higher the number of samples, the more normal it gets
	Central limit theorem is about taking the mean of a bunch of sample distributions, then putting all those means on a distribution. Makes a normal distribution so you can represent data regardless of the original shape of the sample populations

You can:
* On average, how many car deaths are there per year?
* What is the average age of cancer diagnoses?
* How long is the average wait time at a restaurant?
* What is the average length of a domestic house cat?
* What is the average cost of attending college?
<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">The Central Limit Theorem allows us to use normal distributions to understand these questions, largely without regard to the true underlying distributions or other factors.</mark>

It's hard to decide what n is big enough, but n = 30 is a good rule of thumb for a desirable lower bound on sample sizes
