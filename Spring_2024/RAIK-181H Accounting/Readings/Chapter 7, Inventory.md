# THE NATURE OF INVENTORY
**Merchandising firms** buy finished products, warehouse the products for varying periods of time, and then resell the products. They do not manufacture products nor do they consume the products they purchase

## Categories of Inventory
**Merchandise inventory** consists of finished goods that a merchandising company buys from a manufacturing company and makes available to customers

**Manufacturing inventory** consists of three categories:
1. **Raw materials inventory** the raw materials and components
2. **Work-in-process inventory** units of product that have been placed into production but are not fully assembled
	1. *All costs relating to raw materials, labor, factory utilizes, and other factory resources are included in inventory*
3. **Finished goods inventory** units that have been fully manufactured and are ready to be sold to customers
	1. *The cost of each item is accumulated in the work-in-process inventory account and is transferred to the finished goods inventory*

## Costs Included in Inventory
**Purchase price** + other costs such as taxes and shipping

The inventory cost is reduced by **purchase returns and allowances** and by any **purchase discounts**
	Returns and allowances are for things like damaged inventory that reduces the amount owed to the supplier
	In the case of a purchase allowance, the buyer retains the inventory and the amount owed to the seller is reduced by the purchase price

## Inventory Management
**Just-in-case inventory** is a buffer against unforeseen shipping delays and unforeseen demand. Extra inventory
	Reduces risk of running out of inventory, results in **inventory carrying costs** which include insurance, building usage costs, and cost of capital invested in the inventory

**Just-in-time (JIT) manufacturing** minimizes inventory quantities by ordering and stocking only enough to meet immediate demand.
	Reduces inventory carrying costs, increases risk in running out of inventory. Needs more careful planning and management

## Cost of Goods Sold
A company records its costs of goods purchased and adds this to any beginning inventory. This makes up the company's **cost of goods available for sale**. From that total, a company will sell inventory, which is recorded in costs of goods sold. Leftover is ending inventory

## Physical Count of Inventory
Companies need to take **physical count of inventory** at the end of each accounting period
1. Count the number of individual items of inventory available at the end of the period
2. Determine the unit cost of each individual item and multiply the unit cost times the quantity to obtain the total cost for each individual inventory item
3. Add the total cost of all the individual inventory items to obtain the total cost of the aggregate inventory available

If the physical inventory total is less than the Inventory account balance, make an adjustment to increase Cost of Goods Sold and decrease Inventory for the difference. The cost associated with an inventory shortage is known as **inventory shrinkage**
If it is greater than, increase Inventory and decrease Cost of Goods Sold. This is an **inventory overage** cost to Cost of Goods Sold

## Inventory Costing Systems
There's two types of systems, with the difference being how frequently the cost if inventory sold and ending inventory are calculated:
1. **Perpetual inventory system** is when the cost of inventory sold is calculated immediately after each sale
	1. Provides up-to-date balances and increased monitoring control. Spot theft easier and know exactly how much inventory you have on hand
2. **Periodic inventory system** The cost of inventory sold is only calculated periodically at the end of the accounting period
	1. Simple and easy to implement. Attractive for smaller companies who don't need sophisticated tracking systems


# INVENTORY COSTING METHODS
**Goods flow** describes the actual physical movement of inventory through a business
**Cost flow** is the assumed assignment of costs to goods sold and to ending inventory. Often does not reflect the actual goods flow through the business

There are four generally accepted methods of costing inventories:
1. specific identification
2. first-in, first-out (FIFO)
3. last-in, first-out (LIFO)
4. weighted-average cost
We will use all these methods on the below data

The **specific identification method** involves
1. keeping track of the purchase cost of each specific unit available for sale
2. costing the ending inventory at the actual costs of the specific units not sold
![[Pasted image 20240303150652.png]]
You sum the cost of each individual unit not sold to get ending inventory. Subtract from costs of goods available for sale to get cost of goods sold

The **first-in, first-out (FIFO) method** assumes the oldest goods are sold first. The ending inventory is ALWAYS made up of the most recent purchases

The **last-in, first-out (LIFO) method** assumes  the most recent purchases are sold first

The **weighted-average cost method** spreads the total dollar cost of the goods available for sale equally among all units (divide total cost of goods for sale by number of units)

1. Specific identification most closely identifies the actual composition of cost of goods sold and ending inventory
2. FIFO approximates the actual physical goods flow for most firms
3. LIFO is popular because of the income tax savings it provides
	1. The **LIFO conformity rule** requires companies that choose to use LIFO for income tax reporting to also use LIFO for financial reporting to shareholders
4. Weighted-average cost is most often associated with businesses in which undifferentiated goods are commingled in a common area like a warehouse

# LOWER OF COST OR NET REALIZABLE VALUE
When inventory is purchased, it is recorded at its acquisition cost. If the value degrades below it's **net realizable value**, it must be written down to its net realizable value
This is an accounting principle known as the **lower of cost or net realizable value (LCNRV)** rule

Value declines because:
1. It has become damaged or obsolete
2. Market forces, such as lower demand, drive down value

The **net realizable value** is an inventory's item estimated selling price less the expected selling costs

# INVENTORY ANALYSIS
The **gross profit percentage** indicates how much the company earns, on average, for each dollar of sales revenue
$$
\frac{GrossProfitOnSales}{NetSales}
$$

![[Pasted image 20240303152002.png]]
![[Pasted image 20240303152014.png]]
Higher ratio means selling goods at a higher mark-up over cost

The **inventory turnover ratio** indicates how many times a year, on average, a firm sells its inventory
$$
\frac{CostOfGoodsSold}{AverageInventory}
$$
Comes from the income statement and balance sheet. In general, the faster a company can turn over its inventory, the more profitable it is (not always)

**Days sales in inventory** is calculated as
$$
\frac{365}{InventoryTurnover}
$$
