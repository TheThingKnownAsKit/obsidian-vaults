# FINANCIAL REPORTING QUALITY
The structure and content of the financial reports of US public companies are guided by the **conceptual framework**. This is a document created by the FASB that defines objectives and characteristics of financial accounting and reporting

## Objectives of Financial Reporting
1. Facilitate the efficient allocation of resources
	1. Creation of financial reports to provide to investors to assist in investment decisions
	2. Outside investors rely on financial reports to make decisions, so FASB views them as primary users

## Qualities of Useful Accounting Information
1. **Relevance**. To be useful in decision making, it must be relevant to the topic. It must have predictive and/or confirmatory value. Typically interrelated
	1. **Predictive value** is useful in predicting future events
	2. **Confirmatory value** provides feedback about whether financial expectations are met
2. **Faithful Representation.** Financial statements must faithfully report events and transactions to be useful
	1. Information provides substance, it is fairly presented, it is complete, and it is neutral
3. **Comparability.** They must be comparable to other financial statements. It should make like items look alike and different items look different. Not uniformity or necessarily consistency (though consistency is important it's a tool not the goal)
4. **Verifiability.** Financial information needs to be verifiable. Independent observers should reach the same conclusion
5. **Timeliness.** The information needs to be available to users in time to influence their decision
6. **Understandability.** Information should be presented as simply as possible to not be misleading or difficult for the average investor to understand
7. **Conservatism.** Financials should be reported conservatively with a downward bias.
	1. Research suggests this is more valued by users. Accountants are quick to report bad news but slow to report good news. Most investors are concerned about risk. Reduces management's tendency to overreport
8. **Materiality.** An item is considered material if its omission or misstatement is expected to mislead investors. Subjective per company
	1. Misstating Starbuck's earnings by 1k isn't a big deal, but it would be for a small/startup business

# FINDING FINANCIAL ACCOUNTING INFORMATION
Publicly traded companies are required to periodically provide shareholders and potential shareholders with financial statements
	(SEC mandates quaterly reports every 3 months and one annual end of year report)
	Quarterly's are **10-Q's** and annuals are **10-K's**
	Required to be audited by outside source. Contains primary financial statements, footnotes, discussion by management of any major changes.
	Also required to file a **Form 8-K** which discloses events that are expected to affect the value of the company's shares (landing a major contract, outside auditor resigns or is dismissed, etc)
	At annual shareholder meetings, the company must issue **proxy statements** prior to them. Contain information about voting, shareholder proposals, executive compensation
Freely available to the public at http://www.sec.gov/edgar/searchedgar/webusers.htm. Also usually available on company websites

**Management earnings forecasts** are designed to help investors set expectations regarding company performance. Announced a few weeks prior to complete report

# BALANCE SHEET CLASSIFICATION
A **classified balance sheet** presents the assets and liabilities of a business in separate subgroups
![[Pasted image 20240128145435.png]]

## Current Assets
**Current assets** consist of cash and other assets that will be converted into cash or consumed within a normal operating cycle of business or one year, whichever is longer
	Listed in order of expected liquidity

The **normal operating cycle** of business is the average period of time between the use of cash to deliver a service/buy goods for resale and subsequent collection of cash from customers who purchase the services/products. Usually less than a year
	Grocery stores can be anywhere from a week or two to only a day or two for perishable products like bread
![[Pasted image 20240128150720.png]]

## Long-Term Assets
Assets that will not convert into cash / be consumed within the next year/normal operating cycle
Include property, plant, and equipment, intangible assets, and other

### Property, Plant, and Equipment
**Property, plant, and equipment (PP&E)** consists of land, buildings, equipment, vehicles, furniture, and fixtures that are used in day-to-day operations
	Investments in these are called *capital expenditures/investments*
![[Pasted image 20240128150401.png]]

### Intangible Assets
**Intangible assets** consist of brand names, copyrights, patents, and trademarks
![[Pasted image 20240128150333.png]]

### Other Long-Term Assets
Consist of resources consolidated into a single misc category
![[Pasted image 20240128150420.png]]

## Current Liabilities
**Current liabilities** consist of liabilities that must be settled within the normal operating cycle or one year, whichever is longer
Four types:
1. Accounts payable
	1. Owed to vendors and other suppliers for purchases made on credit
2. Accrued expenses payable
	1. Wages, utilities, interest, income tax, and property tax
3. Short-term notes payable
	1. Money owed in a specified formal contract called a note
4. Other current liabilities
![[Pasted image 20240128150647.png]]

## Long-Term Liabilities
**Long-term liabilities** consist of debt obligations not due to be settled within the normal operating cycle or one year
Can include:
1. Long-term notes payable
2. Long-term bonds payable
3. Unfunded employee retirement plans that will be funded in the future

## Stockholders' Equity
**Stockholders' equity** is the residual ownership interest in the assets of a business after its liabilities have been paid off. Divided into two main categories:
1. Amounts invested by the stockholders (common stock)
2. Net income that has not yet been distributed to stockholders as dividends (retained earnings)
![[Pasted image 20240128150943.png]]

## Presentation Format
Two generally accepted formats for balance sheets:
1. The **account form** where assets are displayed on the left side and liabilities and stockholders' equity on the right
2. The **report form** where assets are displayed at the top, liabilities in the middle, and stockholders' equity on the bottom
	1. Most widely used

# INCOME STATEMENT CLASSIFICATION
A **single-step income statement** is the simplest form of an income statement. The sum of the expenses is subtracted from the sum of revenues in a single step to arrive at net income
1. Total revenues
2. Total expenses
3. Revenues - expenses = net income
![[Pasted image 20240128151409.png]]

A **multi-step income statement** presents revenues and expenses in distinct categories to facilitate financial analysis and management decision making
* Provides more information
* No exact format, differs company to company

**Gross profit** is net sales - cost of goods sold. Net profit
**Net sales** record **sales returns and allowances** and **sales discounts**.
	Sales returns and allowances Represent the amount given to the customer because of a refund/product return
	Sales discounts represent an amount allowed to the buyer for early payment

# WORKING WITH FINANCIAL STATEMENTS
## Ratio Analysis
**Ratio analysis** expresses the relation of one accounting number to another through division. Making in a percentage/rate/proportion

 A common ratio is **return on assets (ROA)** equals net income divided by average total assets
	 $AverageTotalAssets=\frac{BeginningTotalAssets + EndingTotalAssets}{2}$
 $ROA=\frac{NetIncome}{AverageTotalAssets}$

To assess companies, one of two techniques is often used to interpret ratios
1. **Trend analysis** compares ratios over time to identify upwards/downwards/stagnant financial trends
2. **Benchmarking analysis** compares ratios to competitors or industry average

## Working with the Balance Sheet
Liquidity and solvency measure a firm's financial well-being. **Liquidity**, in this context, is the ability to pay obligations that come due in the current year. **Solvency** is the ability to pay obligations over the long term

### Liquidity
Factors are level of on-hand cash and how quickly it can generate cash from operations or assets

**Current ratio** is a measure of liquidity. Measures how much cash is available and will become available during the current period
$CurrentRatio = \frac{CurrentAssets}{CurrentLiabilities}$
Bigger ratio = more liquid

### Solvency
Long-term survival

Can be measured using the **debt-to-total-assets ratio** which is exactly what it sounds like. The more debt used to finance day-to-day operations, the higher the risk
$Debts-To-Total-AssetsRatio = \frac{TotalLiabilities}{TotalAssets}$
Bigger ratio = more risk of not being able to pay off its debts aka less solvency

## Working with the Income Statement
### Profitability Analysis Measures
Profitability indicates if a company is efficient enough and if its products/services are valued enough to make a profit

Useful for comparing companies of different sizes. If you use net income, one will dwarf the other in a potentially misleading way. A ratio provides a clearer picture
This is achieved with a profit margin ratio
$ProfitMargin=\frac{NetIncome}{NetSales}$
Bigger ratio = more profitable

## Working with the Statement of Stockholders' Equity
The statement consists of two parts:
1. **Contributed capital** reports the capital contributed by stockholders when they purchase shares
2. **Earned capital** reports capital earned by the company and reinvested into the business (earnings that were not turned into dividends). Retained earnings
