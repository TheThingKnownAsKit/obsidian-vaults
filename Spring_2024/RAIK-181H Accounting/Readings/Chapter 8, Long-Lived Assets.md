# OVERVIEW OF LONG-LIVED ASSETS
Long-lived assets are initially valued based on the asset's historical cost--the cost to acquire and place the asset into service

However, to properly measure income, the costs related to the use of long-lived assets must be matched with the revenues that they help to generate. The portion of an asset's cost that is consumed or used in a period is measured using depreciation expense for physical assets and amortization expense for intangible assets
NOTE: Land does not depreciate and therefore does not require a write-off expense

Companies sometimes report the carrying values of long-lived assets that are leased. These are **Right-of-use assets** and consist of assets such as land, buildings, furniture, and equipment

# ACCOUNTING FOR LONG-LIVED ASSETS
There are four accounting problems long-lived assets have
1. Identifying the type and amount of expenditures that were incurred to acquire the asset
2. Determining the amount of the asset's cost to periodically expense in the income statement. Involves choosing a depreciation method, estimating useful life, and salvage value when asset is disposed of
3. Differentiating between expenditures that are incurred to maintain the asset and expenditures that increase productive capacity or extend useful life
4. Determining the gain or loss to be recognized when a long-lived asset is disposed of

Property improvements that have limited lives are **land improvements**. Like paved parking lots, driveways, sidewalks, fences, etc. Expenditures for these assets are charged to a separate Land Improvement account on the balance sheet and depreciated over the estimated useful life of the improvements
Expenditures to alter or improve leased property are **leasehold improvements**. The extra value provided by the improvements is recorded in the Leasehold Improvements account and depreciated over the life of the lease/improvement, whichever is shorter

# NATURE OF DEPRECIATION
**Salvage value** is the expected net recovery (sales proceeds - disposal costs) when the asset is sold or removed from service. If it is especially insignificant, it can be ignored when computing depreciation under the materiality concept

**Depreciation accounting** is an attempt to allocate the difference between an asset's acquisition cost and its estimated salvage value over its estimated useful life. Not precise, just a way to estimate asset utilization

Methods include:
* Straight-line (most common)
* Declining Balance
* Units-of-production method

## Straight-Line Depreciation
The most commonly used depreciation calculation is the **straight-line method**. Essentially just deciding a per year expense
$$
AnnualDepreciation=\frac{(AcquisitionCost-SalvageValue)}{EstimatedUsefulLife(inYears)}
$$

## Other Methods (Declining Balance and Units-of-Production)
An **accelerated depreciation method** accelerates the amount of depreciation expense recognized in the early years of an asset's life. Assumes productivity is greater because it's new, and should therefore generate more revenue
The **declining-balance method** calculates depreciation expense as a constant percentage of an asset's book value. Over time, book value declines, so the highest expenses would be at the beginning of the asset's life. Therefore, it is an accelerated depreciation 
	Different versions include:
		double-declining balance depreciation which uses a depreciation rate that is twice the straight-line rate
		150 percent-declining balance depreciation uses a depreciation rate that is one and a half times the straight-line rate
Note: Salvage value is not considered in the calculations but the *depreciation of an asset stops when the asset's book value equals its estimated salvage value*

---

The **units-of-production method** allocates depreciation in proportion to an asset's use in operations. Depreciation per unit of production is first calculated by dividing the total depreciable cost of the asset by the asset's projected units-or-production capacity
$$
DepreciationPerUnit=\frac{(AcquisitionCost-SalvageValue)}{TotalEstimatedUnitsOfProduction}
$$
Then, every year you would multiply the number of units sold by the depreciation per unit and use that as the depreciation expense. This continues until the asset has produced the maximum amount of parts it had been estimated to be able to produce

For example, $\frac{(\$1000-\$100)}{45000Units}=\$0.02PerUnit$

## Depreciation Adjustments
If the estimated useful life and salvage value are found to be incorrect, the calculations for depreciation will need to happen
NOTE: When this happens, depreciation estimates will be revised by allocating the undepreciated balance of the asset's book value over the asset's revised remaining useful life

An **impairment loss** is used when a depreciable asset has lost value not related to depreciation such that the expected future cash flows from the asset's use and disposal are LESS than its current book value
	Calculated as the difference between the asset's current book value and its current fair value
	The loss is recorded on the income statement and the asset's book value is reduced

Depreciation used for tax purposes must follow the **modified accelerated cost recovery system (MACRS)**

# REVENUE EXPENDITURES VS CAPITAL EXPENDITURES
**Revenue expenditures** are expenditures relating to property, plant, and equipment that are expensed when incurred. Two common types are:
1. Expenditures for ordinary maintenance and repairs of existing property, plant, and equipment
2. Expenditures to acquire low-cost items that benefit the firm for several periods (paperweights, staplers, etc). Their so low cost that calculating depreciation is inefficient and unnecessary to maintain accurate records. It is immaterial

**Capital expenditures** increase the book value of property, plant, and equipment. To **capitalize** an amount means to classify the expenditure as an asset on the balance sheet. The following are two typical capital expenditures related to property, plant, and equipment:
1. Initial acquisitions and additions
2. Betterments. A **betterment** will either:
	1. Extend the useful life of an asset,
	2. Improve the quality and/or quantity of the asset's output,
	3. Or reduce the asset's operating expenses

# INTANGIBLE ASSETS
Types of intangible assets:
* **Patent**: exclusive privilege granted to an inventor for a period of 20 (usually less, max) years to make, sell, and use an invention
* **Copyright**: gives reproduction rights to the owner of a specific creative work for the author's life + 70 years
* **Franchises**: exclusive rights to operate or sell a specific brand of products in an area
* **Trademarks** and **trade names**: exclusive and continuing right to use certain terms, names, or symbols for branding or product names
* **Goodwill**: the amount paid by one company in acquisition of another company that exceeds the value of identifiable net assets of the acquired company

**Research and development costs** are not added to the balance sheet as an intangible asset because it is highly uncertain whether these expenditures will ultimately result in developing a marketable product

Recorded at historical cost
Amortization of these assets includes:
1. determining the asset's cost
2. estimating the asset's useful life
3. allocating the cost over the asset's estimated useful life
Increases the Amortization Expense account and decrease in assets goes to the Intangible Asset account. It will DIRECTLY decrease the asset account, there is no contra account like accumulated depreciation

Typically uses straight-line amortization
Impairment is the same as long-lived assets
