# PERSISTENT EARNINGS AND THE INCOME STATEMENT
**Persistent earnings** are the extent to which earnings recur over time. Can this company consistently make money? (sometimes called sustainable earnings or permanent earnings)
**Transitory earnings** are non-persistent earnings. Single-period events as special items, restructuring charges, changes in accounting principles, and discontinued operations

Items with higher persistency should be reported higher up on the income statement
	Sales revenue, cost of goods sold, selling, general and administrative expenses, etc
	Regular charges/sales that are consolidated into **net operating income**

Net operating income is then put into **net income from continuing operations before taxes** and **net income from continuing operations** (after deducting income taxes)
![[Pasted image 20240211133320.png]]

One time events are then added/deducted to obtain **net income**

The income statement therefore reports four different income numbers, in descending order of persistence:
1. Net operating income
2. Income from continuing operations before tax
3. Income from continuing operations
4. Net income

In addition, all public companies are required to report net income per common share, referred to as **earnings per share (EPS)** on the income statement immediately following net income

**Discontinued operations** arise when a company sells, abandons, or otherwise disposes of a segment of its operations
Presents two categories of information:
1. The income or loss from the segment's operations for the portion of the year before its discontinuance
2. Any gain or loss from the disposal of the segment
Reported on the income statement immediately after continuing operations (see 11-1)

*Changes in accounting principle implementation must be reported retroactively*. The financial statements of prior years, issued in comparative form with current year financial statements, also must be presented using the new accounting principles as if the new method had been used all along
*Changes in accounting estimation only needs to be reported prospectively*. Only future statements are concerned

A business's **comprehensive income** includes its net income, any changes in the market value of certain marketable securities, and any unrealized gains and losses from translating foreign currency denominated financial statements into US dollars

## Analyzing Firm Profitability
![[Pasted image 20240211135931.png]]

### 1. Gross Profit Percentage
The **gross profit percentage** tells use for every dollar of sales, after the company covers its direct costs, how much is left over to cover indirect costs and leave a profit
**Gross profit/Gross profit on sales** is the difference between net sales and the costs of goods sold and reports the amount of sales revenue after product costs

$GrossProfitPercentage=\frac{GrossProfitOnSales}{NetSales}$
![[Pasted image 20240211135845.png]]

### 2. Profit Margin
**Profit margin** is the ratio that for every dollar of sales, after covering ALL the company's costs, how much was generated for the shareholders?
$ProfitMargin=\frac{NetIncome}{NetSales}$
![[Pasted image 20240211140028.png]]

### 3. Asset Turnover Ratio
The **asset turnover ratio** is for every dollar of assets invested in the company, how many dollars in sales were generated?
$AssetTurnover=\frac{NetSales}{AverageTotalAssets}$
![[Pasted image 20240211140222.png]]

### 4. Return on Assets
**Return on assets** is an overall measure of a firm's profitability. For every dollar of assets invested, how much is generated for the shareholders?
$ReturnOnAssets=\frac{NetIncome}{AverageTotalAssets}$
![[Pasted image 20240211140329.png]]
This summarizes the financial impact of the profit margin and asset turnover ratio. It is the product of these two
![[Pasted image 20240211140409.png]]

### 5. Return on Common Stockholders' Equity
For every dollar of shareholders' equity invested in the firm, how much is generated for the shareholders?
$ReturnOnCommonStockholders'Equity=\frac{(NetIncome-PreferredStockDividends)}{AverageCommonStockholders'Equity}$
![[Pasted image 20240211140559.png]]

## Analyzing Short-Term Firm Liquidity
A firm's **working capital** is the difference between its current assets and current liabilities. Required to repay its current obligations on a timely basis and other
$WorkingCapital=CurrentAssets-CurrentLiabilities$

### 1. Current Ratio
Widely used to measure a firm's ability to meet its current obligations and to have funds available for use in daily operations
$CurrentRatio=\frac{CurrentAssets}{CurrentLiabilities}$
![[Pasted image 20240211140836.png]]

### 2. Quick Ratio
Indicator of the relationship between a firm's liquid, or quick, assets and its current liabilities. It also indicates the financial impact of a company's inventory on its working capital
	Includes cash and cash equivalents, short-term investments, and accounts receivable
	OMITS INVENTORY AND PREPAID ASSETS
$QuickRatio=\frac{CashAndCashEquivalents+Short-TermInvestments+AccountsReceivable}{CurrentLiabilities}$
![[Pasted image 20240211141122.png]]

### 3. Operating-Cash-Flow-to-Current-Liabilities Ratio
Measures a firm's ability to pay its current liabilities as they come due and focuses on a company's operating cash flow. Relates net cash available as a result of operating activities to the average current liabilities outstanding during the period.
	If a company only had it's cash flow from operating, could it pay off it's debt?
$OperatingCashFlowToCurrentLiabilitiesRatio=\frac{CashFlowFromOperatingActivities}{AverageCurrentLiabilities}$
![[Pasted image 20240211141356.png]]

### 4. Accounts Receivable Turnover
The speed with which accounts receivable are collected. Indicates how many times a year a firm collects its average outstanding accounts receivable
$AccountsReceivableTurnover=\frac{NetSales}{AverageAccountsReceivable(net)}$
![[Pasted image 20240211141522.png]]
Higher percentage = faster conversion from receivable into cash

### 5. Average Collection Period
Extension to accounts receivable turnover (4).  The average collection period reveals how many days it takes, on average, for a company to collect an account receivable
$AverageCollectionPeriod=\frac{365}{AccountsReceivableTurnover}$
![[Pasted image 20240211141652.png]]

### 6. Inventory Turnover
indicates whether the inventory on hand is disproportionate to the amount of sales revenue. Excessive inventories not only tie up company funds and increase storage costs but also may lead to subsequent losses if the goods become outdated/unsalable. Higher turnover is preferred (in general)
$InventoryTurnover=\frac{CostOfGoodsSold}{AverageInventory}$
![[Pasted image 20240211141817.png]]

### 7. Day Sales in Inventory
Derived from turnover ratio and reveals how many days it takes, on average, for a firm to sell its inventory on hand
$DaysSalesInInventory=\frac{365}{InventoryTurnover}$
![[Pasted image 20240211141915.png]]

### Operating Cycle
A firm's **operating cycle** is the sum of the days sales in inventory (7) + average collection period (5). It estimates the average time period from the acquisition of inventory, to the sale of inventory, to the eventual collection of cash.

## Analyzing Long-Term Firm Solvency
### 1. Debt-to-Equity Ratio
Evaluates the financial structure of a firm by relating total liabilities to its total stockholders' equity. Considers the extent to which a company relies on creditors versus stockholders to provide financing
$DebtToEquityRatio=\frac{TotalLiabilities}{TotalStockholders'Equity}$
Uses year-end balances and not averages. Structure at a specific point in time. The lower the ratio, the greater protection being provided to creditors and greater flexibility when seeking additional borrowed funds at a low rate of interest
	If the company has to pay out, who has the most protection?

![[Pasted image 20240211142331.png]]

### 2. Times-Interest-Earned Ratio
Evaluates the ability of a company to make its interest payments. Relationship between current interest charges and income
$TimesInterestEarnedRatio=\frac{IncomeBeforeInterestExpenseAndIncomeTaxes}{InterestExpense}$
![[Pasted image 20240211142452.png]]

### 3. Operating-Cash-Flow-to-Capital-Expenditures Ratio
The ability of a firm's operations to provide sufficient cash to replace and expand its property, plant, and equipment. To the extent that acquisitions of plant assets can be financed using cash provided by operating activities, a firm does not have to use other financing sources
$OperatingCashFlowToCapitalExpendituresRatio=\frac{CashFlowFromOperatingActivities}{AnnualNetCapitalExpenditures}$

A ratio in excess of 1.0 indicates that a company has more than sufficient operating cash flow to fund any needed expansion in plant capacity

![[Pasted image 20240211142657.png]]

## Financial Ratios for Common Stockholders
### 1. Earnings per Share
Because stock market prices are quoted on a per-share basis, the reporting of earnings per share of common stock is useful to investors
$EarningsPerShare=\frac{(NetIncome-PreferredStockDividends)}{WeightedAverageNumberOfCommonSharesOutstanding}$
![[Pasted image 20240211142945.png]]

reported on income statement

### 2. Price-Earnings Ratio
Tool for assessing a stock's valuation. Should be interpreted with great caution because of how context sensitive it can be 
Use latest market price per share for the last four quarters
$PriceEarningsRatio=\frac{MarketPricePerShare}{EarningsPerShare}$

![[Pasted image 20240211143142.png]]

### 3. Dividend Yield
Context sensitive. Some people want to buy stocks to sell them at a profit, some people want to buy stocks to be paid dividends. This ratio is for the latter with a higher ratio meaning more dividend yield
$DividendYield=\frac{AnnualDividendPerShare}{MarketPricePerShare}$

![[Pasted image 20240211143332.png]]

### Dividend Payout Ratio
Investors who emphasize yield on their investments would be interested in this ratio. Percentage of net income paid out as dividends to stockholders. Can indicate whether a firm has a conservative of liberal dividend policy and if it's conserving funds for internal growth
$DividendPayoutRatio=\frac{AnnualDividendPerShare}{EarningsPerShare}$

![[Pasted image 20240211143540.png]]
