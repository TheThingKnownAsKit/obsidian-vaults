# RECEIVABLES
**Receivables** are assets representing the right to receive cash or other assets in the future.
* Services/products purchased on credit by customers
* Loans to employees or other companies
* Interest receivable on outstanding loans

**Accounts Receivable** is the current asset created when a company sells goods or services on credit. Increases both Accounts Receivable and Sales Revenue

# LOSSES FROM VALUING ACCOUNTS RECEIVABLE
**Credit-granting policy** is established to decide which customers should be allowed to purchase on credit, and how much credit they should be granted.
	Not everyone will pay off their credit. Businesses usually expect some credit loss

A **credit-collection policy** specifies the credit period and the terms of any credit discounts
	The credit period is the amount of time that customers may take before they are required to pay their outstanding accounts receivable
	A sales discount is the dollar amount that a customer may deduct from the purchase price of goods if they pay within an allowed discount period

Credit losses are an operating expense called **Bad Debt Expense**. Normally a selling expense in the income statement, but some put it as part of their administrative expenses

The process of estimating and recording the bad debt expense for a business is most often executed using the **allowance method**.
	Increase a contra-asset account called the **Allowance for Doubtful Accounts**. Matches credit losses with the related credit sales. Reports accounts receivable at their estimated realizable value

## Percentage of Net Sales Method
The **percentage of net sales method** computes bad debt expense for the period by estimating the amount of credit sales that will be uncollectible
The adjustment results in changes to the doubtful accounts

By focusing on the estimation, this method takes an income statement approach to estimating net realizable accounts receivable

Uses the company's history of credit losses to compute the percentage of credit sales that will be uncollectible. *Credit sales for the period are multiplied by the historical percentage of credit losses*
	For example, credit sales of 500,000 with expected 2% loss = $0.02*500000=10000$
	![[Pasted image 20240225131943.png]]

Only use credit sales, not cash sales (obviously)

It is common for companies to use percentage of net sales method for interim reporting periods, and then apply the aging method (discussed next) at the end of the year. Aging is generally more precise but more costly to implement

## Accounts Receivable Aging Method
The **accounts receivable aging method** computes the desired ending balance in the Allowance for Doubtful Accounts. By focusing on the estimation of the Allowance for Doubtful Accounts, this method takes a balance sheet approach to estimating net realizable accounts receivable

An **aging schedule** analyzes the amount of time that has elapsed since a credit sale originally occurred, which indicates how long a customer's account receivable has remained unpaid
![[Pasted image 20240225132505.png]]
This schedule is then used to compute the probability of non-collection for the receivables in each age category. Percentages are based on past collection experience for similar accounts in similar current economic conditions
This probability of non-collection is then applied to the totals of each age category to determine the appropriate allowance balance

# CREDIT CARD SALES
The issuer of a credit card will charge the seller a fee each time the card is used. The **credit card fee** usually ranges from 1.5-3.5% of the credit card purchase
	Credit card companies charge businesses a fee whenever a customer makes a purchase with their card

Benefits of credit cards:
* The seller does not have to evaluate the creditworthiness of a customer using a credit card
* The business avoids any risk of noncollection since the risk remains with the credit card issuer
* The seller typically receives the cash from the credit card issuer faster than if the customer were directly granted credit by the seller

There are two ways a seller may collect from the credit card issuer:
1. Immediately

# NOTES RECEIVABLE
Notes receivable is used when the credit period is longer than 30-60 days

A **promissory note** is a written promise to pay a certain sum of money on demand or at a fixed future date. Signed by the **maker** and made payable to the order of either a specific **payee** or the **bearer**
![[Pasted image 20240225134030.png]]

A note from a debtor is called a **note receivable** by the noteholder. Usually regarded as a stronger claim against a debtor than an account receivable because the terms of payment are specified in writing

Interest incurred on a promissory note is interest income to the noteholder/payee and interest expense to the maker

## Reporting Notes Receivable on the Balance Sheet
Short-Term Notes Receivable are a current asset. Not readily converted into cash, so below Accounts Receivable

If it is not short term, it is a long term noncurrent asset

Interest Receivable on a note receivable is also a current asset
