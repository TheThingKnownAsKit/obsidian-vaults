# CURRENT LIABILITIES
**Current liabilities** are obligations that will require, within the coming year or the normal operating cycle (whichever is longer), to cause the 
1. use existing current assets
2. or the creatin of other current liabilities

## Accounts Payable
Based on invoices so easy to calculate.

At the end of an accounting period, accountants need to know whether any goods are in transit and what the shipping terms for the goods are
Goods are either shipped free as "free on board" (F.O.B.) shipping point or F.O.B. destination. 
* In F.O.B. shipping point: the buyer takes ownership when the inventory leaves the seller's warehouse
	* An account payable and increase in inventory should be recorded at year-end even if the invoice has not arrived
* In F.O.B. destination: the buyer takes ownership when the goods are delivered

## Notes Payable and Interest
Promissory notes issued when the credit period is longer than 30-60 days

A note can be:
* Exchanged for merchandise
* Substituted for an account payable when an extension is granted
* Loans from bank

Interest on a note is an expense to the maker of the note and recorded under Other Income and Expense category on the income statement. It is NOT an operating expense, that's why it goes here

Interest can be structured in two ways:
1. As an amount paid in addition to the face amount of the note, called the **add-on interest method**, or
	1. Most common and most focused on in this class
2. as an amount included in the face amount of the note, called the **discount method**

### Add-On Interest Method
![[Pasted image 20240324110849.png]]
The interest rate is the annual rate of interest. If a note is given in months, it'll be months/12. If a note is given in days, it is days/360 (or 365, depends on lender)

For example, interest on a three-month note for 4,000 with a nine percent annual interest rate is:
$Interest=4,000*0.09*3/12=\$90$

For example, interest on a 60-day note for 3,000 with a nine percent annual interest rate is:
$Interest=3,000*0.09*60/360=\$45$

# LONG-TERM LIABILITIES
A **bond** is a long-term debt instrument that promises to pay interest periodically as well as a principal amount at maturity, to the bond investor
* In the US, bond interest is usually paid semiannually
* Face value is the principal amount
	* Par value, maturity value

Bonds are usually issued to the general public with a large number of buyers while long-term notes are usually for a single lender
The borrower signs a note payable and the debt is referred to as a **term loan**

Advantages of using bonds and long-term notes instead of common stock include:
1. **No dilution of ownership interest**. Bond and noteholders are creditors, not shareholders
2. **Tax deductibility of interest expense**. Interest expense is a deductible as an expense on a corporation's income tax return. Dividends are not
3. **Income to common shareholders can increase**. Financial **leverage** refers to the use of borrowed funds, particularly long-term debt, to finance a business's growth. When a firm is able to earn a return on its borrowed funds that exceeds the cost of borrowing the funds, it creates income for the shareholders.
	![[Pasted image 20240324112705.png]]

Disadvantages:
1. **Interest expense is a contractual obligation**. Dividends are optional, interest is mandatory
2. **Funds borrowed have a specific repayment date**. Failing to make payments may force the firm into bankruptcy
3. **Borrowing agreement can restrict company actions**. The legal document setting forth the terms of a debt issue is called an indenture. Some provisions may involve restrictions on things like: dividend payments, additional financing, and specific minimum financial rations that must be maintained. These are called debt covenants

## Types of Bonds
* **Secured bonds** pledge specific property as security for meeting the terms of the bond agreement
	* Real estate mortgage bonds (land or buildings)
	* Chattel mortgage bonds (machinery or equipment)
	* Collateral trust bonds (negotiable securities)
* **Debentures** are bonds that have no specific property pledged as a security. They rely on general credit reputation and therefore usually offer higher interest rates
* **Serial bonds** are bonds with staggered maturity dates. For example, a serial bond issue of 15 million may provide for 1 million of the bonds mature each year for 15 years.
	* Bond investors can choose bonds with maturity dates that correspond with their desired length of investment
* **Convertible bonds** grant the bondholder the right to convert the bonds into a company's common stock at some specific exchange ratio
	* Can be a creditor during a certain stage of the firm's life with the option of becoming a stockholder if the firm becomes profitable
	* Lower interest rates
* **Zero-coupon bonds** are bonds that pay no periodic interest but are issued at a substantial discount from their face value
	* The face value is paid to the bondholder at maturity
	* The total interest implicit in the bond contract is the difference between the bond's original issue price and its face value at maturity. For example, a five year 1,000 face value issued for 713 will pay the lender 1,000 at the end of the five years. The total interest is 1,000-713=287
	* Useful when the project provides no immediate cash inflows. Popular with insurance and pension plans

## Bond Features
Bonds with a **call provision** allow the bond issuer to repurchase ("call") the bonds from the other buyer at a pre-specified price. Usually, an extra amount or premium must be paid to the holders

A **sinking fund provision** requires that a borrower retire a portion of its outstanding bonds each year or, in some cases, make payments each year to a trustee who is responsible for managing the funds needed to retire the bonds at maturity. This is generally viewed as making any bond safer for the bondholders

## Bond Prices
Typically sold in units of 1,000 face value and the market price is expressed as a percentage of face value and then sell for that market price
	A 1,000 face value bond quoted at 98% will sell for 980

The amount of the periodic interest payment is determined by the **coupon rate** stated on the bond certificate. Usually annual rates so might need to be converted

The market price is determined by discounting the bond's future cash flows (both its principal and interest payments) to the present using the current **market rate of interest** for the bond as the discount rate. The market rate is the rate of return investors expect on their investment

Bonds sell at face value when the market rate of interest equals the bond's coupon rate
Bonds sell at discount (less than face value) when the market interest rate EXCEEDS the coupon rate
Bonds sell at a premium (more than face value) when the market interest rate is less than the coupon rate

### Interest Expense and Amortization
Each time an interest payment is made, a portion of the bond discount or premium is amortized to interest expense. There are two methods: Straight-line and effective interest 

Straight-line makes the amortization the same each period

GAAP requires the effective interest method, except in cases in which the differences between the two methods is not material. The effective interest method varies the amortization value each period

---

The **straight-line method**:
* If it's a discount: makes the interest expense = the amount of the coupon interest PLUS the discount divided by the number of interest periods
* If it's a premium: it is interest expense = the coupon interest payment LESS the amount of the premium divided by the number of interest periods

The **effective interest method** recognizes a constant percentage of the book value of a bond as interest expense for each interest payment period

The book value of a bond is the balance in Bonds Payable account less the balance in the Discount on Bonds Payable or plus the balance in Premium on Bonds Payable
To obtain interest expense, multiply the book value at the beginning of each period by the effective interest rate
The **effective interest rate** is the market rate of interest used to price the bonds when they were originally issued
The difference between this amount and the amount of interest paid (coupon interest rate x face value) is the amount of discount or premium amortized

### Year-End Adjustments
When a periodic interest payment does not correspond with the fiscal year-end, an adjustment should be recorded reflecting the amount of interest expense incurred but not yet paid. Also includes a pro rata amortization of bond discount or bond premium for the portion of the year involved

# LEASES
The company acquiring the right to use the property (a **right-of-use asset**) is the **lessee** and the owner of the property is the **lessor**

![[Pasted image 20240324121016.png]]

The lessee's accounting for leases in the income statement depends on whether the lease is a **finance lease** or an **operating lease**

## Finance Leases
Transfer CONTROL of the leased asset to the lessee. Equivalent to installment purchases. Mus meet these criteria:
1. The lease transfers ownership of the property to the lessee by the end of the lease term. 
2. The lease contains a purchase option that enables the lessee to acquire the leased asset and the lessee is reasonably certain to exercise the option. 
3. The lease term is a major part of the remaining estimated economic life of the leased asset. 
4. The present value of the lease payments comprise substantially all of the fair value of the leased asset. 
5. The leased asset is very specialized and would be of no use to the lessor at the end of the lease. 

Amortized over the life of the lease and appears on the plant assets in the balance sheet

Total lease obligation is divided between current and long-term liabilities in the balance sheet based on settlement dates

Calculated similar to installment note payments (term loans). Part of each lease payment made by the lessee is charged to interest expense and the remainder reduces the lease obligation

## Operating Leases
Transfer only the USE of the leased asset. The lease expense each period equals the lease payment. No interest expense is related to the lease payments

# CONTINGENT LIABILITIES
A **contingent liability** is a liability in which the obligation to pay a debt depends on future events. Whether or not it is recorded in the accounts depends on the likelihood of the future event occurring and then measuring the obligation

If it will probably occur, the amount of liability can be reasonably estimated and should be on accounts
	For example, product warranty. The analysis assumes that customers are likely to make claims under warranty

If a contingent liability are
1. likely going to happen but are NOT reasonably able to be estimated
2. when the likelihood is reasonably possible but nor probable, regardless of ability to estimate
Then it is not recorded in the accounts but must be disclosed in a note to the financial statements

If the likelihood is remote, it is not recorded nor disclosed. One exception is when a company guarantees the credit of others

## Credit Guarantees
To accommodate important but less financially secure suppliers or customers, a firm may create a **credit guarantee** by cosigning a note payable. Until the original debtor satisfies the obligation, the cosigning firm is contingently liable, even when the likelihood of default is remote
