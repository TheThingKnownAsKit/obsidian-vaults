For an algorithm to be correct, it must:
* Terminate
* Be sound, complete, optimal
* Finds the optimal solution

If an argument contains an assumption, prove the assumption is true too

P problems can be solved in polynomial time
NP is the group of problems that can be verified in polynomial time (it can be proven the sorting algorithm did its work)
There are algorithms that can both be solved and verified in polynomial time (venn diagram intersection)
<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">Most proofs try to prove if their algorithms can be solved, verified, both, or neither in polynomial time</mark>. NP complete are neither, NP hard is just verifiable

Theorems have assumptions and conclusions

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Proof by counter example</mark> an assignment of values to variables that shows that a universal statement is false
	Prove if is true and then is false

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Proof by exhaustion</mark> proving that the conclusion is true for every value possible given value within the interval

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Direct proof</mark> is when you start from known facts & implications, repeatedly apply logical deduction to derive new facts, eventually leading to the conclusion
It follows the format:
	Direct proof. Assume [argument]. We will show that [conclusion]. [Statement of fact]. [Statement of new fact]... [conclusion]

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Proof by contrapositive</mark> to give a direct proof by contrapositive of an implication p -> q, we instead give a proof of the implication $\lnot$q -> $\lnot$p

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Proof by contradiction</mark> is when you assume the negative of the conclusion and derive a logical inconsistency; that is, we assume $\lnot$p and prove False (keep the assumptions true and negative the conclusion)

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Proof by cases</mark> is when you identify a set of cases, and then: you have to 1. prove that in every case the conclusion holds and 2. prove any input fits into one of the cases
	A case is some subset of the universe that has something in common (even, odd)
	(might be easier to prove a proof works on every even and odd integer separately rather than do it at once)
