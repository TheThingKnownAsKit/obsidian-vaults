## Substitution
Chain Rule Anti-Derivative Rule:
$\int f'(g(x))g'(x)fx=f(g(x))+C$

Example of u-substitution
$\int (4x+7)^{12}dx$
$u=4x+7$
$dy=4dx$
$\int \frac{u^{12}}{4}du$
$\frac{(4x+7)^{12}}{52}+C$

Another example:
$\int t^2(t^3-3)^{10}dt$
$u=t^3-3$ note this is nearly t^2
$du=3t^2dt$
$\int \frac{u^{10}}{3}du$
$\frac{1}{3} \int u^{10}du$
$\frac {1}{3} * \frac{1}{11}u^{11}+C$
$\frac{1}{33}(t^3-3)^{11}+C$

Example with a definite integral:
$\int_0^2xe^{-x^2}dx$
METHOD ONE: Convert everything to terms involving u:
$u=-x^2$
$du=-2xdx$
Evaluate what u equals at the endpoints.
u(0) = 0, u(2) = -4
Use these as the new endpoints
$\int_0^{-4}-\frac{1}{2}e^udu$
$-\int_{-4}^0-\frac{1}{2}e^udu$
$\frac{1}{2}\int_{-4}^0e^udu$ 
Anti-derivative of e^u is just e^u, so evaluate $\frac{1}{2}e^u$ with both endpoints and subtract them
$-\frac{1}{2}[\frac{1}{e^4}-1]$
METHOD TWO: Find the indef integral first
We did that earlier
$-\frac{1}{2}\int e^udu$
$-\frac{1}{2}e^u+C=-\frac{1}{2}e^{-x^2}+C$
Use fundamental theorem of calculus and the easiest form of the antiderivative (C = 0) and evaluate at 0 and 2
$-\frac{1}{2}[e^{-4}-e^0]=-\frac{1}{2}[\frac{1}{e^4}-1]$
