# 1. Principles and Practice of Economics
**Positive economics** describes what people actually do or predicting what people might do based on previous data. Analytical and data driven
**Normative economics** recommends what people should do. Very subjective. When economic analysis is used to help individual economic agents choose what is best in their personal interest, it is *prescriptive economics*.

**Microeconomics** is the study of how individuals, households, firms, and governments make choices, and how those choices affect prices, resource allocation, and other agents.
**Macroeconomics** is the study of the economy as a whole. Growth rate, inflation rates, unemployment rates, etc.

**Budget constraint** is the set of things that a person can choose to do or buy without breaking their budget.

Cost-benefit analysis produces the best **Net benefit**, which is the sum of the pros + the cons of this option.

# 2. Economic Methods and Economic Questions
Economists use the scientific method to:
1. develop models
2. test models with data

Two main reasons why correlation might not equal causation:
1. omitted variables
	1. Something has been left out of the study that would explain why they're correlated. The actual cause
2. reverse causality

**Randomization** is the assignment of subjects to an experiment by random.
A **natural experiment** is an empirical study in which some process, out of control by the experimenter, has assigned subjects to control and treatment groups in random.

Good economic questions have these two properties:
1. *Good economic questions address topics that are important to individual economic agents and/or society*. Economic studies generally study how to contribute to society welfare with positive implications
2. *Good economic questions can be answered*. Economics is not philosophy

# 3. Optimization: Doing the Best You Can
*Economists use optimization to predict most of the choices that people, households, businesses, and governments make.*
Worth noting that economists don't assume that people always SUCCESSFULLY optimize, most people don't. This is still worth using as an approximation even if it is inconsistent

**Behavioral economics** explains why people optimize in some situations and fail to optimize in others. It makes models using economic and psychological theories of decision making
Common situations where people fail to optimize are:
* Self-control problems (procrastination, addiction)
* When someone is new to a task

**Optimizing using total value** has three steps:
1. Translate all costs and benefits into common units, like dollars per moth
2. Calculate the total net benefit of each alternative
3. Pick the alternative with the highest net benefit

**Optimization using marginal analysis** breaks an optimization problem down by thinking about how costs and benefits change as you hypothetically move from on alternative to another. You only think about the differences
	Often faster to implement because it does not consider ALL the pros/cons, just the ones that differ
	*It will never change the ultimate answer to the question "what is optimal?" but it will clarify the way you think about optimization*
A **marginal analysis** is a cost-benefit calculation that focuses on the difference between one feasible alternative and the next
	Total value and marginal analysis should result in the same answer
	It has three steps:
	1. Translate all costs and benefits into common units, like dollars per month
	2. Calculate the marginal consequences of moving between alternatives
	3. Apply the Principle of Optimization at the Margin by choosing the best alternative
The **marginal cost** is the extra cost generated by moving from one feasible alternative to the next
The **Principle of Optimization at the Margin** states that an optimal feasible alternative has the property that moving to it makes you better off and moving away from it makes you worse off
