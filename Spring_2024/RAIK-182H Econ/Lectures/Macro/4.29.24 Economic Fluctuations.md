This model includes <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">frictions</mark>, which are things such as:
* Job search: Workers must work to find the right match
* Efficiency wages: A wage premium intended to increase workers' productivity (paid extra to improve moral and productivity)
* Legal restrictions: Policies such as the minimum wage might create a wedge between willingness to work and hire

Here, $\overline w$ is the price floor imposed by minimum wage. It is the people who want to work but can't get a job because of the price floor

Another friction is <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">downward wage rigidity</mark>. Essentially the concept that workers do not like it when they are paid less. Famously, workers are often more sensitive to cuts in nominal wages than real wages (average people don't take into account inflation when pay changes)
	This is why most firms tend to lay off workers rather than cut pay. If fired people lose morale, it's not your problem and the people working for you are neutralish

# KEYNESIAN, MONETARIST, REAL BUSINESS THEORY
The critical driver of the macroeconomy is the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">aggregate demand</mark>, the economy's overall demand for goods and services. This is important because it drives investment and hiring decisions

The concept of <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">multipliers</mark> has important implications for the consequences of an aggregate demand shock.
	A downturn in aggregate demand triggers layoffs, which means fired workers spend less, which reduces aggregate demand, which triggers more layoffs, etc
	ON TOP OF THAT, asset prices fall, homeowners and debt havers default, which means less money for creditors, which means banks may struggle to give people the money they're owed and will pull back on loans, which means less people have access to the immediate relief loans can offer, etc

# Phillips Curve
Posits that as unemployment rises, the inflation rate falls
![[Pasted image 20240429133250.png]]

There are some exceptions (dots)
<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Stagflation</mark> of the 1970s meant that inflation was high but so was unemployment. Shortcoming in Keynesian school of thought. This sparked a new school of thought kicked off by Milton Friedman (<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Monetarists</mark>) that emphasizes the importance of money supply. They blamed the stagflation on the Fed's management of M (money supply in equation)

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Real business cycle</mark> school of thought focuses on supply factors. In particular, the idea that technology causes fluctuations. Therefore, if technology change is the root cause, there is less reason for government intervention. This school of thought has less explanatory power for typical economic contraction
