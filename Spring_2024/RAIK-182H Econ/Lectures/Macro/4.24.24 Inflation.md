# INFLATION
Inflation can be measured with a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">price index</mark> that capture average price changes in a basket of goods. Number not %



One way to explain inflation is the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">quantity theory of money</mark>. Quantity of money means the current money supply (M2) aka currency in circulation, savings accounts, and most bank accounts.
	A building block of this theory is the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">velocity of money</mark>. This is the number of times a dollar is used for transaction in a period of time. How often is it changing hands?
$$
TotalExpenditure\to M*V=P*Y\gets TotalProduction
$$
Where m is the money supply, v is the velocity of money, p is the price level (cpi, some index of prices), and y is real gdp
The left side is total expenditure and the right side is total income/production

The quantity theory relies on two observations:
1. Velocity of money is relatively stable from year to year
2. Real GDP is also fairly stable
<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">THEREFORE, this means that when money supply rises, prices must rise too</mark>

A related expression is the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">inflation equation</mark>. Same thing but in %
$$
\%\Delta P = \%\Delta M-\%\Delta Y
$$
Where p is inflation, m is money supply, and y is real gdp



<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Seignorage</mark> is government revenue from printing money. When overused this can lead to inflation because consumers have more money to spend which increases demand which increases quantity and price
	Not common. Really only used when there is no other source of revenue for the government to use
Not the only way to increase money supply



Costs/benefits of inflation:
* <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Logistical costs</mark>: High inflation leads to "menu costs" for firms (literally having to keep printing menus because prices are rising, and similar)
* <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Confuses price signals</mark>: Markets use prices to coordinate, but firms may find it difficult to separate changes in relative prices from changes in the overall price level
* <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Redistribution</mark>: Inflation redistributes from savers to borrowers (pro and con depending). If inflation outpaces your loan interest then you're paying less
* <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Government revenue</mark>: If inflation comes from the government printing money (seignorage), it generates revenue which is good when managed
* <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Stimulus</mark>: If workers' wages are "downwardly rigid", inflation reduces firm costs and may stimulate investment and hiring in the short run

# THE FEDERAL RESERVE SYSTEM
The Federal Reserve is the US' <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">central bank</mark>. It is a government institution that monitors financial institutions, controls key interest rates, and indirectly controls the money supply

The fed is set up to be politically independent. It has 12 branches, it is self-funded, and it operates mostly autonomously from Congress and such. Positions appointed by the President but still 99% autonomous

The official mission of the fed is the dual mandate of:
* Maintain price stability (inflation should be low and predictable, 2% every year target)
* Maximum sustainable levels of employment

They achieve this through three main activities
* <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Regulation</mark>: Auditing bank financial statements, engaging in "stress tests" to ensure bank assets are not too risky
* <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Interbank transfers</mark>: Overseeing the system that allows banks to process transactions
* <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Managing macroeconomic fluctuations</mark>: Manipulating the total quantity of bank reserves to affect interest rates, inflation, and unemployment <--- main point

For banks to do their job, they need liquidity: funds available immediately. If reserves are low, they turn to the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">federal funds market</mark> which allows banks to lend to one another
This is modeled as the market for total reserves. Y-axis is federal funds rate and x-acis is quantity of reserves. Demand side is represented by banks who want to lend, build up a safety net. Federal funds rate is the interest rate on these loans (overnight loans)
