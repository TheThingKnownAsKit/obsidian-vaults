**Simultaneous Games** are ones where decisions need to be made at the same time
A **Sequential game** allows choices to play out over time. Things like turned based games basically

**Asymmetric information** is when all the players involved in a game do not understand everything at play. They're actually playing on the left but they think they're on the right

**Bertrand competition** is when firms compete by setting prices. Usually when price is all that matters in competition and not things like quality or differences. The **Nash equilibrium** is then where price equals marginal cost

For **extensive-form games**, you need a different way to model them than the punnett square. One way to do this is using a **game tree** which is exactly what it sounds like (each choice makes a branching path. players alternate). Each point where a decision must be made is a **node**.
	Can have first mover advantage, second mover disadvantage (comes from flexibility), or neither

The best way to solve a game tree is from a **backward induction**. Start from the end and work your way backwards to the choices that would produce that outcome. Prune based on desired outcomes

---

One way to preserve higher prices is **collusion** where firms conspire to set Q or P. To collude,
1. Need to be able to detect and punish cheaters
2. Need to ensure there is long run value

This can work through **grim strategy** which is to plan to price at marginal cost forever if my competitor cheats. Basically you go "if I can't make a profit neither can you" and destroy the profitability of the market as a punishment for cheating

Can also do it through more subtle strategies such as "price match guarantees" reduce incentive to cut prices. If you guarantee that you will pay whatever the lowest competitor price a consumer can find at a competitor's store, it encourages the competitor to not cut their prices
Also tacit agreements to differentiate products where you don't sell exactly the same product as your competitor.
