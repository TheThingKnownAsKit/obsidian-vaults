# PRODUCTION FUNCTION AND INTRO
Firms' goal is to maximize profit by producing output using **physical capital**--goods, machines, buildings, money--and **labor**.

These inputs are combined using a **production function** where it'll be like $Q=\sqrt{L}$. Remember things like specialization, law of diminishing returns, and negative marginal product

# MARGINAL PRODUCT OF LABOR GRAPH
Hirer's Problem is similar to the buyers problem. You can determine how much labor to use by finding the **marginal product of labor** which tells you how much output you'd get from another worker-hour. The **value of marginal product of labor** is MPL x output price to get the value of the products produced, translates to dollars
![[Pasted image 20240401130442.png]]

Suppose it costs 10 dollars to hire another worker, then we have the graph
![[Pasted image 20240401130614.png]]
The L is how many workers, so one worker, two workers, three workers, etc. *The optimal number of labor force is where the hourly wage intersects the VMPL*.

Therefore, the VMPL also represents the **labor demand**. Equivalent to a demand curve in a lot of ways. This would turn the price axis into a wage axis

# LABOR-LEISURE TRADEOFF/BUDGET CONSTRAINT GRAPH
The **labor-leisure tradeoff** is similar to a buyer choosing how much to get of two goods. It is the tradeoff for working vs not working. The budget constraint's slope is equal to the hourly wage - the opportunity cost of leisure. More leisure = less working. *The optimal number of hours is where the indifference curve intersects the budget constraint*
![[Pasted image 20240401131447.png]]
![[Pasted image 20240401131515.png]]
T is the maximum amount of hours in a day. You can't work more than 24 hours, it's not possible.

The amount of labor you do is T - leisure.
The amount of leisure you do depends on the indifference curve.

# SUPPLY AND DEMAND GRAPH
Put labor demand with labor supply (number of workers available) to find the supply and demand graph for the labor market
![[Pasted image 20240401132302.png]]

Does not account for: varying quality of employees, varying quality of workplaces beyond wages, there are contracts and NOT free entry/exit, searching for a job/employee costs, employee benefits like health care and retirement plans

**Labor-complementary technologies** increase the marginal product of labor, increase labor demand. **Labor-saving technology** reduces the marginal product of labor, decreases demand
Things can be neither, and things can be substitutes that completely eliminate a job
These things shift labor demand

Things that shift labor supply are:
Changes in population, worker preferences and tastes (impacts labor-leisure tradeoff), and changes in opportunity cost (what else could you be doing if you weren't working?)
