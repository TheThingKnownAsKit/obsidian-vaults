A *derivational morpheme* changes the meaning and/or part of speech of a root.
	Adding -un to the word do changes the meaning
	Adding -ish to a noun creates adjectives
They carry semantic meaning and are like the affix version of content words.

When a new word is created through derivation, other possible derivations may be blocked.
	Communist exists, therefore we don't need communite or communian.
Some derivations trigger pronunciation changes, while others do not.
	Specific -> specificity, or bake -> baker

*Inflectional morphemes* have only grammatical function (similar to function words) and never change the part of speech of the root.
	waited, waits, waiting
Always suffixes in English and always follow any derivational morphemes
	commit + ment can be become commit + ment + s but not commit + s + ment

Inflectional morphemes are *productive*, meaning they apply freely to almost any appropriate root morphemes.
	Almost all nouns take the suffix s to make a plural (some use ize)

There are exceptions and *suppletion's*
	Not all words undergo regular morphological processes (feet, went, sing, children)
	When new words enter the language, regular morphological rules generally apply to them 
	Borrowed words may retain borrowed morphology 

*Lexical Gaps* (accidental gaps): words that could be in a language but aren't
	Some permissible sound sequences have no meaning (blick)
	Speakers can distinguish between impossible words and possible but non-existing words (curiouser, linguisticism, antiquify)
	The morphological component of our mental grammar consists of not just a lexicon but also of rules that enable us to create and understand new words, and to recognize possible and impossible words.
