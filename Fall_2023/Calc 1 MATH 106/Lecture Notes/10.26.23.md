## L'Hopital's Rule
(Lo ha-pe-tal)
Let f and g be differentiable at x = a, and suppose that f(a)=g(a)=0 and g'(x) != 0 for x near a. Then
$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(a)}{g(a)}$ Weird L thing over = $\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}= \frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}$
*THIS RULE ONLY APPLIES IF YOU'RE DIVIDING $0\over0$ or $\infty\over\infty$*
If f and g are differentiable and both approach zero or both approach +- infinity as x -> a (where a is allowed to be +- infinity), then the rule applies.
*You essentially continue to take the derivative until it is no longer 0/0 or infinity/infinity.*

THIS IS NOT THE SAME AS TAKING THE DERIVATIVE OF A COMPOSITE FUNCTION CONTAINING F(A)/G(A). Because quotient rule makes it more than just f'(a)/g'(a).
Note: The small circled "L" over the equals sign indicates that L'Hopital's rule is being applied.

Example: (0/0)
Calculate $\lim_{t\to 0} \frac{e^t-1-t}{t^2}$
If t is 0, then you're dividing by 0. 
Apply the rule.
$\lim_{t\to 0} \frac{e^t-t}{2t}$
Still 0/0. Apply again.
$\lim_{t\to 0} \frac{e^t}{2}$ is no longer 0/0 and it simplifies to 1/2.

Example: (infinity/infinity)
Basically if you have an equation with an infinite domain divided by one with an infinite domain and you're taking the limit as x -> infinity.
Keep taking the derivative until you either don't have an infinity domain, OR you have a constant divided by a function. This is basically the equivalent to constant/infinity which is equivalent to 0 since any constant divided by infinitely large numbers will get very close to 0.

The solution to these is when you can simplify it to constant/infinity or similar OR you can solve it into having no x's.

$\lim_{x\to \infty} \frac{3x^2+5}{15-6x^2}$
$\lim_{x\to \infty} \frac{6x}{-12x}$
$\lim_{x\to \infty} \frac{6}{-12} = -\frac{1}{2}$
