# Second Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
Second fundamental theorem
$\frac{d}{dx}[\int_a^xf(x)dt]=f(x)$
For example,
$\frac{d}{dx}[\int_0^x7e^{2t}dt]=7e^{2x}$

If the x is not by itself in the bounds, like x^2, you need to use the chain rule
This is f(x^2). Chain rule
$\frac{d}{dx}[\int_a^{x^2}\sin(t^2)dt]=\frac{d}{dx}F(x^2)=F'(x^2)*2x$
$=sin(x^2)^2*2x$

You can ignore the lower bound if it is a number. It CANNOT have x or a variable in it. You will need to isolate it.
$\frac{d}{dx}[\int_x^{10}ln(1+t^4)dt]$
$\frac{d}{dx}[-\int_{10}^xln(1+t^4)dt]=-ln(1+x^4)$

# Integration by Substitution (U-Substitution)
Opposite of chain rule.
Essentially, find u (inner function) and du (derivative of inner function) and rewrite in terms of u and du. Take the integral which the u makes much simpler. Replace u with it's original value.

When finding du, for some reason you should isolate the variable as much as possible. So if it's $du=2xdx$, change it to $\frac{1}{2}du=xdx$
Figured it out, this is because the derivative of du needs to match the given outside function. The original for the above example was $xe^{x^2}$ and 2x does not match x.

Substitution is not always possible if the derivative of the inside function is not a scaler multiple of the outside.

**Using substitution to find DEFINITe integrals**
One of the following strategies:
* Compute the indefinite integral by itself first, expressing an antiderivative in
* Convert the original limits to new limits in terms of the new variable and do not convert the antiderivative back to the original variable.
Example:
Evaluate $\int_1^3\frac{dx}{5-x}$
$u=5-x, du=-1dx->-du$
So the integral is $\int\frac{-du}{u}$. Evaluate bounds
To find the new bounds, evaluate u at each bound.
	When x = 1, u = 4
	When x = 3, u = 2
	These are the new bounds
Also put the negative outside to simplify
$-\int_4^2\frac{du}{u}=-\int_4^2u^{-1}du$
Bounds are upside down bc negative
$-(\ln|2|-\ln|4|)$
No need to switch back to x's.
