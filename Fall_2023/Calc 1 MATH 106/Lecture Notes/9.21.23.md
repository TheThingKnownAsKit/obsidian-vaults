## Derivatives of Functions Given Implicitly
$x^2+y^2=2^2$ circle with radius 2
It's two graphs put together. If you solve for y it's $y=\sqrt{4-x^2}$ OR $y=- \sqrt{4-x^2}$
It's the bottom half of a circle together with the top half.

An *implicit equation* gives you the value of whatever you're looking for, but not necessarily solved for it. Like the circle equation above, you could find y even though it's not equal to y.
Instead of dealing with the square root, you can use *implicit differentiation* by taking the derivative of the equation before setting it equal to y.
$2x+2y(\frac{dy}{dx})=0$
	You chain rule the y^2 because remember it's d/dx of y with respect to x. It's not the same as just taking the derivative of x with respect to x3.
$2y(\frac{dy}{dx})=-2x$
$(\frac{dy}{dx})=\frac{-2x}{2y} = -\frac xy$

Another example: Consider 2y = x^2 + sin(y) (This can't be solved for y). Find y'
$2y=x^2+\sin(x)$
$2y'=2x+\cos(y)y'$
$2y'-\cos(y)y'=2x$
$(2-\cos(y))y'=2x$
$y'=\frac{2x}{2-cos(y)}$

Example: Find the equation of the tangent line to the curve at (-1, 2), where $x^2-xy+y^2=7$
$2x-(1*y+x*y')+2y*y'=0$
$2x-y-xy'+2yy'=0$
$(-x+2y)y'=-2x+y$
$y'=\frac{-2x+y}{(-x+2y)}$
Tangent line to graph of f(x) at x=a. This is the derivative at y=f'(a), or the slope.
$y=f'(a)(x-a)+f(a)$ tangent line equation
Point is (-1,2) and we just found slope
$y'=\frac{(-2)(-1)+2}{-(-1)+2(2)}=\frac{4}{5}$
$y=\frac{4}{5}(x+1)+2$ is the equation for the tangent line.

Implicit differentiation gives us one of the most powerful tools in differentiation.
Differentiate $y=x^x$
The ordinary rules of derivatives do not apply.
$\ln(y)=\ln(x^x)$
$\ln(y)=x\ln x$      Take the derivative
$\frac{1}{y}*y'=1*\ln(x)+x*\frac 1x$
$\frac 1y *y'=\ln(x)+1$
$y'=y(\ln(x)+1)$
$\frac{d}{dx}[x^x]=x^x(\ln(x)+1)$    Put y = x^x back into the equation. Implicit differentiation allows us to solve equations you can't with normal differentiation rules.
