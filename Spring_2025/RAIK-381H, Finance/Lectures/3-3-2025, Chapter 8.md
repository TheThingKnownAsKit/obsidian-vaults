You can measure how risky a stock is with
$$s=\sqrt{ \frac{\sum^N_{i=1}(x_{i}-\overline{x})^2}{N-1} }$$

You can measure how risky a stock portfolio with only 2 options is with
$$\text{Portfolio Variance}=w^2_{A}*\sigma^2(R_{A})+w^2_{B}*\sigma^2(R_{B})+2*(w_{A})*(w_{b})*Cov(R_{A},R_{B})$$
Where wA and wB are portfolio weights, $\sigma^2(R_{A})$ and $\sigma^{2}(R_{B})$ are variances, and Cov(RA, RB) is covariance

Obviously this is wildly complicated so there is a much easier way to do it in excel

Bad risk in investments is diversifiable risk. Firm-specific risk that can't be diversified away through holding more stocks
Good risk is undiversifiable risk. Economy-wide risk that can't be diversified away through holding more stocks

As an investor, your goal is to maximize the return per unit of risk. The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Sharpe Ratio</mark> measures return versus volatility (risk)
$$\frac{E(R)-Rf}{\sigma}$$
Where E(R) - Rf is expected returns and sigma is volatility
Use =stdev() to compute sigma

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)</mark> is the grandparent of finance formulas
$$E(R_{i})=R_{f}+\beta_{i}*MRP$$
Where
- $E(R_{i})$ is the stock i's expected return
- $R_{f}$ is the risk free rate
- $\beta_{i}$ is the amount of the stock's systemic risk
- MRP is the market risk premium
	- Captures the difference between aggregate stock market returns and the risk free rate (Rm - Rf)
	- Usually measured with historical data
	- From 1927-2024 it has been about 8%

Example: what is the expected return on a stock with a beta of 2?
$Rf+2*MRP=Rf+2*(Rm-Rf)$

<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">This is a very popular model that doesn't actually work that well in practice. </mark>It is mostly used in corporate finance to determine the expected return on equities and less so for investing
