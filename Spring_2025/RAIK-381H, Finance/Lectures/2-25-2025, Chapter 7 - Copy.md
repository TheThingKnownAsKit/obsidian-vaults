## Bonds
<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Par/Face Value</mark> is the value of the bond that will be paid when it matures
	Usually 1,000 in this class
<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Coupon interest rate</mark> is the annual interest rate paid on the bond before it matures
	Almost all bonds are paid semiannually (half of the annual interest rate)
<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Maturity date</mark> specifies when the bond will be repaid
	Most 10-40 years

Bond ratings (on how likely they are to fail) go:
AAA > AA > A > BBB > BB > B
Anything above BBB or more is investment grade, the rest is junk

Typically, as bond price goes up, bond yield goes down, and vice versa

Types of bonds:
- Treasury
	- Perceived as risk-free since governments dont typically default on debt
- Corporate
	- Riskier for poorly run & highly levered (has a lot of debt) firms
- Municipal (muni)
	- Issued by state & local governments
	- Unlike treasury and corporate bonds, interest earned from municipal bonds is free from federal taxes (so rich people like them)
- Foreign
	- Issued by foreign governments or corporations

If the bonds price is LESS than its par value, it is <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">discounted</mark>
	coupon rate < discount rate
If the bonds price is GREATER than its par value, it is <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">premium</mark>
	coupon rate > discount rate
If the bonds price is EQUAL to its par value, it is trading <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">at par</mark>
	coupon rate = discount rate

Premium bonds are typically more attractive because of the higher coupon rates. The interest rate usually dictates the return

You can lose money on bonds in two ways:
1. Default risk (the firm or government defaults)
2. Interest rate risk
	1. If prevailing interest rates fluctuate you can lose money
	2. If you lock in an interest rate, and then the rate for other people fluctuates, your bond loses value and you lose money

$$Price=\sum^n_{i=1}\frac{\text{coupon rate}}{(1+\text{interest rate})^i}$$
