## Parametric Lines in R^2
<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Parametric equations</mark> are equations that tell you where t is on a curve, and t is called a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">parameter</mark>

The direction vector of a parametric equation is the slope. Can lay on the line or be parallel and it will still be valid

Let $v=\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b\end{bmatrix}$ be a direction vector for line l, and let $(x_{0},y_{0})$ be an arbitrary point on l. Then the following parametric equations describe l:
$$x=at+x_{0}$$
$$y=bt+y_{0}$$

## Parametric Lines in R^N
You can generalize to:
Let $v=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1} \\ v_{2} \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ v_{n}\end{bmatrix}$ be a direction vector for line l in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}$ be an arbitrary point on l. Then the following parametric equations describe l:
$$x_{1}=v_{1}t+a_{1}$$
$$x_{2}=v_{2}t+a_{2}$$
$$\dots$$
$$x_{n}=v_{n}t+a_{n}$$

This is significant because this means <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">you can write systems of linear equations with just a direction vector and a point</mark>. For example, direction vector $v=\begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 8 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}$ and passes through the point (1, -3, 1)
Following the form outlined above, you know that $x=v_{1}t+a_{1}\to x=4t+1$. <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">The point gives you all the a's and the direction vector gives you all the slopes</mark>

Instead of writing out each equation, we summarize this information into a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">vector equation</mark>
Let $v=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1} \\ v_{2} \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ v_{n}\end{bmatrix}$ be a direction vector for line l in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}$ be an arbitrary point on l. Then the following parametric equations describe l:
$$x(t)=\begin{bmatrix}
v_{1} \\
v_{2} \\
. \\
. \\
. \\
v_{n}
\end{bmatrix}t+\begin{bmatrix}
a_{1} \\
a_{2} \\
. \\
. \\
. \\
a_{n}
\end{bmatrix}$$

## Planes in R^3
A nonzero vector n is called <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">normal</mark> to the plane if it is orthogonal to every vector in the plane. It makes a right angle with the plane (goes straight up or down)

Let $d=ax_{0}+by_{0}+cz_{0}$, then this gives us the standard form of the equation of a plane:
$$ax+by+cz=d$$
The plane through $P_{0}(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})$ with a normal vector $n=\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b \\ c\end{bmatrix}\ne 0$ is given by:
$$a(x-x_{0})+b(y-y_{0})+c(z-z_{0})=0$$

Given a normal vector and a point, you can create the system of linear equations. The point will give you all the $var_{0}$ and the normal vector will give you a, b, c, etc

The equation of the plane through the point $P_{0}=(1,-1,3)$ with a normal vector $n=\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$ is
$$\begin{matrix}
3(x-1)-1(y-(-1))+2(z-3)=0 \\
3x-y+2z=10
\end{matrix}$$

## Hyperplanes in R^N
Lines and planes may be very different but they are all graphs of linear equations. Lines are just 2D equations, planes are 3D, hyperplanes are 4D or more

In general, an equation of the form $a_{1}x_{1}+a_{2}x_{2}+\dots+a_{n}x_{n}=d$ is called a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">linear equation</mark>.

The graph of a linear equation is called a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">hyperplane</mark> for n > 3
