## Standard Unit Vector
A <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">unit vector</mark> is a vector of length 1
The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">standard unit vectors</mark> in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are the vectors
$$\overrightarrow{e}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
\dots \\
0 \\
1 \\
0 \\
\dots \\
0
\end{bmatrix}$$
Where 1 is in the ith component and 0 is everywhere else

<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">Common standard unit vectors you should memorize:</mark>
$$e_{1}=i=\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
0 \\
0
\end{bmatrix}\text{x axis}$$
$$e_{2}=j=\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
1 \\
0
\end{bmatrix}=\text{y axis}$$
$$e_{3}=k=\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
0 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}=\text{z axis}$$
Note: i and j can exist in 2D space, just drop the 3rd 0. k can only exist in 3D space

A <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">linear combination</mark> is a combination of the standard unit vectors added/subtracted and multiplied by scalar values?

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Normalization</mark> is when you calculate a unit vector in the direction of another vector v
1. Find the magnitude of v, $\|v||$
2. Multiply the vector by $\frac{1}{||v||}$

## The Dot Product
The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Dot product</mark> of vectors x and y in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is
$$\overrightarrow{x}\cdot \overrightarrow{y}=\begin{bmatrix}
x_{1} \\
x_{2} \\
. \\
. \\
. \\
x_{n}
\end{bmatrix}\cdot \begin{bmatrix}
y_{1} \\
y_{2} \\
. \\
. \\
. \\
y_{n}
\end{bmatrix}=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+\dots+x_{n}y_{n}$$
Basically you just multiply each ith element and sum everything

Vectors are <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">orthogonal</mark> if their dot product equals 0. Basically means they cancel each other out

## Orthogonal Projection
A <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">direction vector</mark> of a line L is any vector that lies on L. Basically it's just going in the same direction as a line
The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">projection</mark> of a vector v onto a nonzero vector d is given by
$$proj_{\overrightarrow{d}}\overrightarrow{v}=\frac{\overrightarrow{v}\cdot\overrightarrow{d}}{\overrightarrow{d}\cdot\overrightarrow{d}}\overrightarrow{d}$$
Note that this is also the projection of v onto a line L with a direction vector d

### Orthogonal Projection Example
![[Pasted image 20250127121246.png]]

We need to find the direction vector d. To do this, find the starting point that the blue and red line intersect, which is (-2,-2), and when d would reach another findable point, in this case (0,-1) and put it in vector form
$$\overrightarrow{d}=\begin{bmatrix}
x_{2}-x_{1} \\
y_{2}-y_{1}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
0-(-2) \\
-1-(-2)
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}$$

With the vector we already have, we can compute the projection
$$proj_{\overrightarrow{d}}\overrightarrow{v}=\frac{\begin{bmatrix}
4 \\
6
\end{bmatrix}\cdot \begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}}\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}=\frac{14}{5}\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{28}{5} \\
\frac{14}{5}
\end{bmatrix}$$

With this projection we can find the distance from the point (2,4) to the line L. You can do this by:
$$\overrightarrow{v}_{\perp}=\overrightarrow{v}-\overrightarrow{v}_{||}=\begin{bmatrix}
4 \\
6
\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{28}{5} \\
\frac{14}{5}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
-\frac{8}{5} \\
\frac{16}{5}
\end{bmatrix}$$

Then, compute the distance/magnitude
$$||v_{\perp}||=\sqrt{ \left( -\frac{8}{5} \right)^2+\left( \frac{16}{5} \right)^2 }=\frac{8\sqrt{ 5 }}{5}$$

### Summarized Orthogonal Projection Steps
1. You need two points that the line goes through. This can be given through a graph or explicitly in the question. Use the two points to compute:
$$\overrightarrow{d}=\begin{bmatrix}
x_{2}-x_{1} \\
y_{2}-y_{1}
\end{bmatrix}$$
2. You will be given a starting vector (or the points to find the vector). Take that vector (v used as a placeholder) and the direction vector from step 2 and compute the projection:
$$proj_{\overrightarrow{d}}\overrightarrow{v}=\frac{\overrightarrow{v}\cdot\overrightarrow{d}}{\overrightarrow{d}\cdot\overrightarrow{d}}\overrightarrow{d}$$
NOTE: Once you compute the fraction, it will become a scalar value, so you just do scalar multiplication with the standalone d
NOTE: You can also find a d by finding the unit vector of v

Sometimes this is all the question wants, sometimes it also wants distance to a point. Stop here if all the question wants is the projection (which will be in matrix form). Continue if you need distance to a point

3. Take the original given vector v and the projection calculated previously and subtract them (with regular matrix subtraction)
$$\overrightarrow{v}_{\perp}=\overrightarrow{v}-\overrightarrow{v}_{||}$$
Note: sometimes instead of $v_{\perp}$ it's just called d for distance

4. Finally, compute the distance/magnitude normally on $\overrightarrow{v}_{\perp}$
$$\|v_{\perp}\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+...+v^2_n}$$
This is the distance from vector v to the line L
