## Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
Let A be a square n x n matrix. We say a nonzero vector x is an <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">eigenvector</mark>
 of A if $A\overrightarrow{x}=\lambda\overrightarrow{x}$ for some scalar $\lambda$
 - Basically, if you can multiply a matrix by a vector and get the vector * a scalar as the result, it is an eigenvector with the eigenvalue of the scalar
 - Another way to think about it is first you multiply the matrix by the vector is:
   Did you receive the vector back?
	 - If yes, this is the eigenvector with an eigenvalue of 1
	 - If no, can you factor a constant out of the resulting matrix to equal the initial vector? Sometimes this is easy, sometimes you need to use the characteristic equation
		 - If yes, this is the eigenvector with the eigenvalue as the factored out constant
		 - If no, this is neither the eigenvector or eigenvalue

We call $\lambda$ the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">eigenvalue</mark> associated to the eigenvector x

If x is an eigenvector of matrix A and $\lambda$ is the corresponding eigenvalue, then <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">every scalar multiple of x is also an eigenvector of A and</mark> $\lambda$ is the corresponding eigenvalue

Algorithm for solving for eigenvectors using given eigenvalues:
1. Calculate $A-\lambda I$
2. Solve $A-\lambda I$ into RREF
3. The solution for the variables is each row value of the eigenvector for this specific eigenvalue
4. Repeat per eigenvalue
	1. Note if it's a 2x2 matrix don't even bother just solve it like a two-variable equation

### Example, Finding Eigenvectors Given Eigenvalues
To find the eigenvectors for the matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ given eigenvalues $\lambda=1,3$

1. Calculate $A-\lambda I$, in this case lambda is 1 so it's just minus the identity matrix
$$\begin{bmatrix}
2 & 1 \\
0 & 2
\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
2-1 & 1 \\
1 & 2-1
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}$$
2. Put this in RREF
$$\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}\overrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\begin{bmatrix}
1&1 \\
0&0
\end{bmatrix}$$
3. These variables represent the eigenvector for this eigenvalue
Putting this equal to 0, you get $x_{2}=1$ and $x_{1}+x_{2}=0\to x_{1}=-x_{2}\to x_{1}=-1$
So, the eigenvector at eigenvalue 1 is $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}s$ for any s (the s is here because of the free variables)
4. Do all this again for eigenvalue 3
$$A-\lambda I=\begin{bmatrix}
-1 & 1 \\
1 & -1
\end{bmatrix}$$
$$\text{RREF}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 \\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}$$
Free variable of T
$$\text{Eigenvector}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}t$$
for any t


## The Characteristic Equation and Finding Eigenvalues
Consider an arbitrary n x n matrix A, if $A\overrightarrow{x}=\lambda\overrightarrow{x}$, then
$$\begin{matrix}
A\overrightarrow{x}-\lambda\overrightarrow{x}=\overrightarrow{0} & \Rightarrow  & A\overrightarrow{x}-\lambda I\overrightarrow{x}=\overrightarrow{0} \\
 & \Rightarrow & (A-\lambda I)\overrightarrow{x}=\overrightarrow{0} \\
 & \Rightarrow & \text{Has nontrivial solution for }\overrightarrow{x} \\
 &  & \text{if and only if }\det(A-\lambda I)=0
\end{matrix}$$

The equation $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ is called the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">characteristic equation</mark> of A
We let $\lambda$ be a variable here and call the polynomial $\det(A-\lambda I)$ the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">characteristic polynomial</mark> of A
The roots of the characteristic equation are the eigenvalues of A

Example:
If you have the matrix $R_{x}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}$
First, calculate $R_{x}-\lambda I$
$$R_{x}-\lambda I=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & -1
\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda & 0 \\
0 & \lambda
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
1-\lambda & 0 \\
0 & -1-\lambda
\end{bmatrix}$$
$$\det(R_{x}-\lambda I)=(1-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)\Rightarrow \lambda=1,-1$$

## Eigenspaces
Let A be an n x n matrix and let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of A. Then the set of all eigenvectors associated with $\lambda$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$

The set of all eigenvectors associated with a given eigenvalue of a matrix is known as the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">eigenspace</mark> associated with that eigenvalue

Let $S_{\lambda}$ be the eigenspace of a matrix A corresponding to an eigenvalue $\lambda$. Then, $S_{\lambda}=\text{null}(A-\lambda I)$
