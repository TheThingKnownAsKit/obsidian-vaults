## Similar Matrices
We say two n x n matrices A and B are <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">similar</mark>, denoted $A\sim B$, if $A=PBP^{-1}$ where P is some invertible matrix

For example, $B=\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ and $P=\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $P^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
$A=PBP^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ -5 & 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, so matrix A is similar to B

Theorem: If $A\sim B$ are n x n matrices, then
- $\det(A)=\det(B)$
- $rank(A)=rank(B)$
- A and B have the same characteristic polynomial and eigenvalues
- $A^{-1}\sim B^{-1}$ (if A is invertible)
- $A^T\sim B^T$
- $A^k\sim B^k$ for all integers $k\geq1$

## Diagonalizable Matrices
Let A be an n x n matrix. A is a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">diagonalizable</mark> if it is similar to a diagonal matrix. That is, $A=PDP^{-1}$ where P is some invertible matrix and D is a diagonal matrix

For example, $A=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 4 & -1 \\ -2 & -4 & 4\end{bmatrix}$ is diagonalizable with $P=\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -2\end{bmatrix}$, $D=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 6\end{bmatrix}$, and $P^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{4} & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{4} \\ \frac{1}{2} & 1 & \frac{1}{2} \\ \frac{1}{4} & \frac{1}{2} & -\frac{1}{4}\end{bmatrix}$

An n x n matrix A is diagonalizable if and only if there is an invertible matrix P given by $P=\begin{bmatrix}| & | &  & | \\ x_{1} & x_{2} & \dots & x_{n} \\ | & | &  & |\end{bmatrix}$, where the columns $x_{i}$ are eigenvectors of A. Moreover, if A is diagonalizable, the corresponding eigenvalues of A are the diagonal entries of the diagonal matrix D

Observe: A diagonalizable $\Leftrightarrow$ eigenvectors of A form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$

### Example with a Diagonalizable Matrix
$A=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 4 & -1 \\ -2 & -4 & 4\end{bmatrix}$

First, find $\det(A-\lambda I)$

$A-\lambda I=\begin{bmatrix}2-\lambda & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 4-\lambda & -1 \\ -2 & -4 & 4-\lambda\end{bmatrix}$

$\begin{matrix}\det(A-\lambda I)&=&(2-\lambda)[(4-\lambda)^2-4]-0+0 \\ &=&(2-\lambda)(\lambda^2-8\lambda+12) \\ &=&(2-\lambda)(\lambda-2)(\lambda-6)\end{matrix}$
$\lambda=2,2,6$

Second, solve $A-\lambda I$ for each eigenvalue, put it into RREF, and write it as a basis

$A-2I=\begin{bmatrix}0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ -2 & -4 & 2\end{bmatrix}\overrightarrow{RREF}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\to \begin{matrix}x_{1}=-2s+t \\ x_{2}=s \\ x_{3}=t\end{matrix}$
$\text{Basis for }S_{\lambda=2}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}\right\}$

$A-6I=\begin{bmatrix}-4 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & -2 & -1 \\ -2 & -4 & -2\end{bmatrix}\overrightarrow{RREF}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\to \begin{matrix}x_{1}=0 \\ x_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}s \\ x_{3}=s\end{matrix}$
$\text{Basis for }S_{\lambda=6}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ -\frac{1}{2} \\ 2\end{bmatrix}\right\}$

Third, the basis for these two eigenvalues form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$, and if you put the eigenvalues together you can find D and if you put the basis together you can find P
$D=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 6\end{bmatrix}$
$P=\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$

### Example with a Not Diagonalizable Matrix
$B=\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$

First, solve for the eigenvalues
$B-\lambda I=\begin{bmatrix}-\lambda & 1 \\ 0 & -\lambda\end{bmatrix}\to \det(B-\lambda I)=\lambda^2$
$\lambda=0,0$

Second, solve for the eigenvectors
$B-0I=\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\to \begin{matrix}x_{1}=s \\ x_{2}=0\end{matrix}$
$\text{Basis for }S_{\lambda=0}=span\left(\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}\right)$ which is NOT a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$ so B is not diagonalizable

## Algebraic and Geometric Multiplicity
The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">algebraic multiplicity</mark> of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the number of times it appears as a root in the characteristic polynomial

The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">geometric multiplicity</mark> of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is $dim(S_{\lambda})$
