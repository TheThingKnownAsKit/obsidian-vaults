## A Brief Introduction to R^N
The set of all real numbers is denoted by $\mathbb{R}$. Geometrically this is the familiar number line/plane

$\mathbb{R}^2$ is the set of ordered pairs (x,y) where $x, y \epsilon \mathbb{R}$. This is the cartesian plane
	Means x, y in R

Similarly, $\mathbb{R}^3=\{(x,y,x):x,y,z\epsilon \mathbb{R}\}$ is just 3D space
	Notation means the set of ordered triples z, y, z with x, y, z in R

The subdivisions formed by the coordinate plane are called <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">octants</mark>. 

## Distance in R^N
Distance in $\mathbb{R}^2=\text{Pythagorean Theorem}$

Let A and B be points in $\mathbb{R}^3$
$$AB=\sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2+(y_1-y_2)^2+(z_1-z_2)^2}$$
This can be generalized to $\mathbb{R}^n$
$$AB=\sqrt{(a_1-b_1)^2+(a_2-b_2)^2+...+(a_n-b_n)^2}$$

## Introduction to Vectors
A <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">scalar</mark> is a quantity that has size, often called magnitude, but no direction
A <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">vector has both size and direction</mark>. Notation of $\overrightarrow{u}$ **u** or $\overrightarrow{AB}$ (a vector that starts at point A and ends at B. Ais the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">tail</mark> and b is the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">head</mark>).

<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">Vectors are positionless</mark>. Vectors with the same size and direction are equivalent

A vector in <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">standard position</mark> is if the tail is the origin. Since vectors are determined ONLY by their size and direction, we often refer to them by their standard position
	You observe the slope of the vector and it's value is it's head coordinates if its tail was at the origin. For example, vector that goes up 1 over 2 as the slope and starts at 0,0 will end at (2,1), so it's value is $\begin{bmatrix}{} 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

$\begin{bmatrix}{} 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ 2 is the first component, 1 is the second component. This is the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">component form</mark>. This is a column vector, but the form [2,1] is exactly the same and is just a row vector

<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">To find standard form,</mark>
$$\overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{bmatrix}{} b_1-a_1 \\ b_2-a_2\end{bmatrix}$$
Can be generalized to
$$\overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{bmatrix}{} b_1-a_1 \\ b_2-a_2 \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ b_n -a_n\end{bmatrix}$$

A vector $v\epsilon\mathbb{R}^n$ in standard position whose head is at $(v_1,v_2,...,v_n)$ can be written in component form as $\begin{bmatrix}{} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ v_n\end{bmatrix}$

## Basic Vector Math
The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">length</mark> (also called size or magnitude) of a vector is the distance between its head and tail

Let $v=\begin{bmatrix}{} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ v_n\end{bmatrix}$ be a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$, then the length or magnitude of v is
$$\|v\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+...+v^2_n}$$

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">ADDITION</mark>: where u and v are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$
$$u+v=\begin{bmatrix}{} u_{1} \\ u_{2} \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ u_{n}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}{} v_{1} \\ v_{2} \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ v_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
u_{1}+v_{1} \\
u_{2}+v_{2} \\
. \\
. \\
. \\
u_{n}+v_{n}
\end{bmatrix}$$
u_{1}+v_{1}\\ u_{2}+v_{2}\\ . \\ . \\
<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Scalar multiplication</mark> (just multiplying a vector by a constant)
$$ku=k\begin{bmatrix}{} u_{1} \\ u_{2} \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ u_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{} ku_{1} \\ ku_{2} \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ ku_{n}\end{bmatrix}$$
