## Abstract Vector Spaces
Let V be a nonempty set. The set V is a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">vector space</mark> provided that V is closed under addition and scalar multiplication and the following properties for vectors $\overrightarrow{u},\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{w}\in V$ and scalars $k,p\in \mathbb{R}$:

Consider the set of all m x n matrices $\mathbb{M}_{m,n}$. It is closed under addition and scalar multiplication. Further note for matrices $A,B,C\in\mathbb{M}_{m,n}$ and scalars $k,p\in \mathbb{R}$
![[Pasted image 20250331114236.png]]
Hence, $\mathbb{M}_{m,n}$ is a vector space

More examples of valid vector spaces:
- The set $\mathbb{P}^2$ containing polynomials in a variable x of degree 2 or fewer
- $\mathbb{P}^n$ is a vector space for any positive integer n

## Subspaces
A subset U of a vector space V is called a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">subspace</mark> of V if U
1. contains the zero vector of V
2. is closed under addition
3. is closed under scalar multiplication
For example, $\mathbb{P}^2$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{P}$

### Example
Show the set $U=\{p(x)\in\mathbb{P}|p(3)=0\}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{P}$
(this basically means that U is the set of polynomials which have 3 as a root)

1. Should contain the zero vector
$0(x)=0\in u$ since $0(3)=0$ then U contains the zero vector of V
2. Closed under addition
$P(x),q(x)\in u$ recall that $(p+q)(x)=p(x)+q(x)$
Therefore, $(p+q)(3)=p(3)+q(3)=0+0=0$
So it is closed under addition
3. Closed under scalar multiplication
$k\in \mathbb{R}$
$kp(3)=k*0=0$
Closed under multiplication

Therefore, U is a subspace of $\mathbb{P}$

## Linear Combinations
Let V be a vector space, $v_{1},\dots,v_{n}$ be vectors in V, and $a_{1},\dots,a_{n}$ scalars. Then, 
$$a_{1}\overrightarrow{v}_{1}+a_{2}\overrightarrow{v}_{2}+\dots+a_{n}\overrightarrow{v}_{n}$$
is a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">linear combination</mark> of the vectors $v_{1},\dots,v_{n}$

## Span
Let V be a vector space. The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">span</mark> of vectors $v_{1},\dots,v_{n}$ in V is the set of all linear combinations of these vectors, written $span(\overrightarrow{v}_{1},\dots,\overrightarrow{v}_{n})$
Let U be a subset of V. If $U=span(\overrightarrow{v}_{1},\dots,\overrightarrow{v}_{n})$, then we say U is spanned by $\overrightarrow{v}_{1},\dots,\overrightarrow{v}_{n}$

Theorem: <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">Let S be any subset of a vector space V. Then u=span(S) is a subspace of V</mark>

## Linear Independence
Let V be a vector space. Vectors $v_{1},\dots,v_{p}$ in V are <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">linearly independent</mark> if the only solution to
$$a_{1}\overrightarrow{v}_{1}+a_{2}\overrightarrow{v}_{2}+\dots+a_{p}\overrightarrow{v}_{p}=\overrightarrow{0}$$
is the <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">trivial solution</mark> $a_{1}=a_{2}=\dots=a_{p}=0$
Otherwise, we say $v_{1},\dots,v_{p}$ is <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">linearly dependent</mark>

## Bases
Let V be a vector space

A set $\mathcal{B}$ of vectors are a <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">basis</mark> for V if
1. $V=span(\mathcal{B})$
2. $\mathcal{B}$ is linearly independent

Theorem 9.1.23: Let $\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}$ be a basis for V. Then every element $v\in V$ can be written as a unique linear combination of $\mathcal{B}$

## Dimension
Theorem: Suppose $\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\}$ and $\mathcal{L}=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{t}\}$ are bases of V. Then $s=t$
The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">dimension</mark> of V is the number of elements, n, in any basis for V and we write $dim(V)=n$

## Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces
A vector space is said to be <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">finite-dimensional</mark> if it is spanned by finitely many vectors

Lemma 1: Let V be a vector space spanned by n vectors. If a linearly independent subset S of V contains m vectors, then $m\leq n$
Lemma 2: Let $\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}$ be a linearly independent subset of V. If u is not in $span(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})$, then $\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k},u\}$ linearly independent

Theorem: <mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">Any linearly independent subset can be expanded to a basis</mark>

## Coordinate Vectors
Let $\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\}$ be a basis for a vector space can V.
By Theorem 9.1.23, every vector $v\in V$ can be written uniquely as
$$\overrightarrow{v}=a_{1}\overrightarrow{b}_{1}+a_{2}\overrightarrow{b}_{2}+\dots+a_{n}\overrightarrow{b}_{n}$$
for some scalars $a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\in \mathbb{R}$

The <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">coordinate vector</mark> with respect to $\mathcal{B}$ is $[\overrightarrow{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1} \\ a_{2} \\ . \\ . \\ . \\ a_{n}\end{bmatrix}$
